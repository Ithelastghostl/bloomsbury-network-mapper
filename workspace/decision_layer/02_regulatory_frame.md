# 02 — UK Regulatory Frame: Charity Prospect Research & Wealth Screening

**Phase R2 output** | Accessed: 2026-05-05 | Covers: UK GDPR (as retained in DPA 2018), PECR 2003 (as amended by DUA Act 2025), ICO enforcement, Fundraising Regulator Code 2025

---

## 1. Lawful Basis: Legitimate Interest under Article 6(1)(f)

**ICO has stated** that legitimate interest (LI) is the most flexible lawful basis and is likely to be most appropriate where data is used in ways individuals would reasonably expect and that have a minimal privacy impact. (ICO, "What is the legitimate interests basis?", ico.org.uk, accessed 2026-05-05)

For prospect research and wealth screening, **industry guidance suggests** that charities will typically rely on LI rather than consent, because obtaining prior consent before researching a prospect is often impractical. (Fundraising Regulator, GDPR Briefing: Corporate Fundraising, fundraisingregulator.org.uk, accessed 2026-05-05)

**ICO has stated** that if PECR requires consent for an outreach channel, you cannot substitute legitimate interest for that channel's data-processing step — the stricter rule applies. (ICO, "Sending direct marketing: Choosing your lawful basis", ico.org.uk, accessed 2026-05-05)

**Note on Recognised Legitimate Interest (RLI):** The DUA Act 2025 introduced a category of "recognised legitimate interest" for specific purposes. ICO published guidance in late 2025; direct marketing and prospect research do not currently fall within the RLI schedule, so the three-step LIA remains required. (Clifford Chance, "UK ICO's Recognised Legitimate Interest Guidance", cliffordchance.com, accessed 2026-05-05)

## 2. The Three-Step LIA Test

**ICO has stated** that before relying on legitimate interest, you must conduct and document a Legitimate Interest Assessment (LIA) covering all three steps. (ICO, "Legitimate interest assessment (LIA)", ico.org.uk, accessed 2026-05-05)

### Step 1 — Purpose Test
Ask: is the interest genuine and legitimate? You must identify:
- **What** you are trying to achieve and **who benefits** (the charity, the cause, or the public)
- Whether the interest is lawful and not contrary to law or public policy
- For prospect research: the interest is identifying donors capable of making major gifts to advance charitable objects — ICO guidance recognises direct marketing as a legitimate interest in principle, but it is not automatic

Key questions: Would a reasonable person recognise this interest as legitimate? Is there a clear and real benefit?

### Step 2 — Necessity Test
Ask: is processing the personal data actually necessary to achieve the purpose?

**ICO has stated** that necessary "doesn't mean it has to be absolutely essential," but you must show it is "a targeted and proportionate way of achieving your purpose." (ICO LIA guidance, ico.org.uk, accessed 2026-05-05)

Key questions:
- Could a less privacy-intrusive method achieve the same result?
- Are you collecting the minimum data needed?
- For wealth screening: is passing donor records to a third-party screener the only practicable way to assess financial capacity?

### Step 3 — Balancing Test
Ask: do the individual's interests, rights, or freedoms override the charity's interest?

**ICO has stated** this is "a light-touch risk assessment to check that any risks to individuals' interests are proportionate." (ICO search results, ico.org.uk, accessed 2026-05-05)

Factors that weigh **against** the charity:
- The individual would not reasonably expect this use of their data
- Processing involves sensitive inferences (financial capacity, wealth level)
- The individual is a new contact who has no existing relationship with the charity
- The data was sourced from third parties without the individual's knowledge
- Processing could cause distress, embarrassment, or reputational harm

Factors that weigh **for** the charity:
- Existing donor relationship creates a reasonable expectation of continued contact
- Processing uses publicly available information only
- Clear privacy notice describes the practice and offers easy opt-out
- Impact on individual is low (no automated decision with legal effect)

**ICO has stated** that when relying on legitimate interest, you take on extra responsibility for protecting rights, and making sure individuals "reasonably expect what you do" is central to the lawful basis. (ICO, "What is the legitimate interests basis?", ico.org.uk, accessed 2026-05-05)

**Documentation requirement:** The LIA must be written and retained as part of your accountability obligations. A template is provided by ICO (ico.org.uk/media2/for-organisations/forms/2258435/gdpr-guidance-legitimate-interests-sample-lia-template.docx).

## 3. DPIA: When Required, What Triggers It

**ICO has stated** that a DPIA is mandatory before starting any processing "likely to result in high risk." Two or more of the nine WP248/EDPB criteria generally require a DPIA; in some cases one criterion alone is sufficient. (ICO, "When do we need to do a DPIA?", ico.org.uk, accessed 2026-05-05)

### Three statutory triggers under UK GDPR Article 35(3)
1. Systematic and extensive profiling with **significant effects** on individuals
2. Large-scale processing of **special category** or criminal offence data
3. Systematic monitoring of a **publicly accessible area** on a large scale

### The nine EDPB/ICO criteria (WP248rev01)
A DPIA is indicated when processing involves two or more of:
1. Evaluation or scoring (including financial capacity scoring)
2. Automated decision-making with legal or significant effects
3. Systematic monitoring
4. Sensitive data or data of a highly personal nature
5. Data processed on a large scale
6. Datasets that have been matched or combined from multiple sources
7. Data concerning vulnerable data subjects
8. Innovative use of technology or novel application of existing technology
9. Processing that prevents data subjects from exercising a right or using a service

**ICO has additionally stated** two UK-specific criteria requiring a DPIA in combination with EDPB criteria: use of innovative technology, and profiling or special category data to decide access to services. (ICO, keepabl.com summary of ICO criteria, accessed 2026-05-05)

### Application to wealth screening and prospect research

A typical wealth screening operation — sending donor records to a third-party firm that combines them with property registers, company filings, and social data to produce a wealth score — engages **at minimum criteria 1, 5, and 6** (scoring, large-scale, data matching). This is very likely to require a DPIA. The CASE guidelines (industry guidance) reached the same conclusion: LIAs and DPIAs "where needed" are a baseline for lawful prospect research. (CASE Guidelines on GDPR Compliance, case.org, accessed 2026-05-05)

**NOT SOURCED — flag as open question:** Whether the ICO's formal high-risk list published under Article 35(4) specifically names donor wealth screening. The ICO's list has not been publicly updated beyond the statutory triggers above, but the criteria combination analysis makes a DPIA strongly advisable.

## 4. Privacy Notice Requirements

**ICO has stated** that under Articles 13 and 14 of UK GDPR, privacy notices must include as a minimum: (ICO, "What privacy information should we provide?", ico.org.uk, accessed 2026-05-05)

| Element | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Identity and contact details of controller | Always required |
| Purposes of processing | Always required |
| Lawful basis relied on | Always required |
| Legitimate interests (when relied on) | Must describe the specific interest |
| Recipients or categories of recipient | Must name third-party screeners or categories |
| Transfers to third countries | Required if applicable |
| Retention period | Always required |
| Right to object to direct marketing | Must be "explicitly brought to attention" and presented separately |
| Source of data (when not from subject) | Required under Article 14 |
| Categories of data obtained from third parties | Required under Article 14 |

### Timing
- Data collected directly from the individual: notice provided **at the time of collection**
- Data obtained from third parties (e.g., a prospect identified via research): notice provided **within one month** of obtaining data (Article 14(3)(a))

### Prospect research specifics
**Industry guidance suggests** that if a privacy notice clearly states that prospect research and wealth screening are conducted, and that notice is "easily available to donors and others," prospect research can be fair processing. (Fundraising Regulator / CIoF GDPR briefings, fundraisingregulator.org.uk, accessed 2026-05-05; CASE guidelines, case.org, accessed 2026-05-05)

**ICO has stated** that the right to object to direct marketing is absolute and must be honoured without delay. Where you rely on legitimate interest for any other processing, individuals have a right to object that you must consider and can only override with compelling legitimate grounds. (ICO, "Right to object", ico.org.uk, accessed 2026-05-05)

## 5. PECR: Outreach Channel Constraints

PECR 2003 (as amended) imposes channel-specific consent rules that sit **above** UK GDPR. **ICO has stated** that where PECR requires consent, you cannot rely on legitimate interest as the lawful basis for that channel. (ICO, "Sending direct marketing: Choosing your lawful basis", ico.org.uk, accessed 2026-05-05)

| Channel | Rule | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Email/SMS to individuals** | **Consent required** (PECR Reg. 22) | No LI route for unsolicited electronic mail to individual subscribers |
| **Email/SMS — soft opt-in** | LI permissible if prior customer relationship, product/service similar to what prompted that relationship, opt-out offered at collection and in every message | Standard rule; charity can use this for existing donors re similar activities |
| **Email/SMS — charitable purposes soft opt-in** (new, DUA Act 2025, Reg. 22(3A)) | LI permissible for charities where: (a) contact details obtained via expression of interest in/support for charitable purposes; (b) message solely furthers charitable purposes; (c) opt-out offered at collection and in every message | Charities only; does not extend to commercial activity |
| **Email/SMS to corporate subscribers** | LI permissible without prior consent for communications relating to their work role | Applies to trust staff, company representatives; not personal donations via work email |
| **Live telephone calls** | LI permissible unless TPS/CTPS registered (then consent required) | Must check TPS/CTPS before calling |
| **Automated calls** | Consent required | No LI route |
| **Post** | LI permissible (no PECR rule); UK GDPR LIA still required | Must screen against MPS if using bought lists |
| **SMS to individuals** | Consent required (same as email) | No LI route |

**Key implication:** Cold email to individual prospect donors requires consent under PECR regardless of how robust the LIA is. The charitable soft opt-in (DUA Act 2025) provides some relief for existing supporter relationships but does not cover cold outreach to newly identified prospects.

## 6. The 2017 ICO Enforcement Actions — Lessons for This Project

### What happened
In December 2016 (with wider enforcement continuing into 2017), the ICO fined the RSPCA £25,000 and the British Heart Foundation £18,000 under the **Data Protection Act 1998**. A subsequent wave of enforcement resulted in eleven further charities being fined. (Civil Society, civilsociety.co.uk, accessed 2026-05-05; RPC Legal, rpclegal.com, accessed 2026-05-05)

### The three violations found
The ICO found three categories of breach, all violations of DPA 1998 Principles 1 (fair and lawful) and 2 (purpose limitation):

1. **Wealth screening without disclosure.** Charities passed donor records — names, addresses, dates of birth, donation amounts, Gift Aid status, donation method — to third-party wealth management firms. Those firms combined the data with property registers, public records, and social data to produce financial capacity scores. Donors were never told. ICO found this processing was not disclosed and therefore not fair.

2. **Tracing / telematching.** When donor contact details were incomplete, charities hired firms to fill gaps using existing identifiers (old phone numbers traced to new ones, email addresses matched to postal addresses). Again, not disclosed to donors.

3. **Data sharing / data pooling.** Charities exchanged donor records with other charities, creating pooled datasets for prospecting. No donor consent or disclosure.

### Root cause (per ICO findings)
**ICO stated** that "none of the supporters...were told about these uses of their personal data, and so the supporters were not given the opportunity to consent or object." (Mills & Reeve summary, mills-reeve.com, accessed 2026-05-05) Fair processing statements required review to be "explicit, clear, transparent and highly visible."

### Legislative note
The enforcement was under DPA 1998, not UK GDPR (which entered force May 2018). The transparency and purpose limitation principles are substantially reproduced in UK GDPR Articles 5(1)(a) and 5(1)(b). The same conduct today would engage those articles plus the requirement to conduct an LIA, likely a DPIA, and the obligation to provide an Article 14 notice within one month.

### Direct lessons for this project
- Prospect research and wealth screening are lawful **only if disclosed** in a visible, accessible privacy notice before or at the point of use
- Passing any donor data to a third-party screener without a DPA and without disclosure is the same violation the ICO fined charities for in 2016–2017
- Tracing / address-matching services carry the same risk as wealth screening and must be disclosed
- Data pooling with other organisations requires either consent or a separately assessed LI basis with disclosure

## 7. Third-Party Processor Obligations

When a charity engages a prospect research firm or wealth screening provider that processes personal data **on the charity's behalf**, the firm is a **data processor** under Article 4(8) UK GDPR and the charity remains the **data controller**.

**ICO has stated** that Article 28 requires a written contract covering: (ICO, "What needs to be included in the contract?", ico.org.uk, accessed 2026-05-05)

1. Process data only on documented controller instructions
2. Bind processor staff to confidentiality
3. Implement appropriate technical and organisational security measures (Article 32)
4. Not engage sub-processors without prior written controller authorisation; impose same obligations on sub-processors
5. Assist controller in responding to data subject rights requests
6. Assist controller in meeting security, breach notification, DPIA, and prior consultation obligations
7. At contract end, delete or return all personal data and certify deletion
8. Provide all information necessary to demonstrate compliance; submit to audits

**ICO has stated** that before contracting, the controller must conduct **due diligence** on the processor to verify it will implement appropriate technical and organisational measures, proportionate to the risk. (ICO, accountability framework contracts guidance, ico.org.uk, accessed 2026-05-05)

**Industry guidance suggests** the due diligence process should include: reviewing the processor's security posture, checking whether they hold ISO 27001 or equivalent certification, establishing what sub-processors they use (e.g., data aggregation services), and confirming their data retention and deletion practices. (Factary, Guide to GDPR-Compliant Wealth Screenings, factary.com, accessed 2026-05-05)

**Practical note:** If the wealth screening firm also acts as a **co-controller** (e.g., it enriches data for its own purposes), a different legal arrangement — a joint controller agreement under Article 26 — is required. This must be verified in the procurement stage.

## 8. Summary: What This Means for a UK Charity Running Prospect Research

A compliant prospect research programme requires all of the following:

| Requirement | Binding or Best Practice | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Written LIA completed and retained before processing | **Binding (UK GDPR Art. 6(1)(f))** | ICO, ico.org.uk |
| DPIA completed for large-scale or scored wealth profiling | **Binding (UK GDPR Art. 35)** where 2+ criteria met | ICO, ico.org.uk |
| Privacy notice discloses: prospect research, wealth screening, third-party recipients, Art. 14 notice to new prospects within 1 month | **Binding (UK GDPR Arts. 13–14)** | ICO, ico.org.uk |
| Right to object to direct marketing explicitly flagged in notice | **Binding (UK GDPR Art. 21)** | ICO, ico.org.uk |
| Article 28 DPA in place with every third-party screener before data transfer | **Binding (UK GDPR Art. 28)** | ICO, ico.org.uk |
| Due diligence on processor security conducted | **Binding (accountability principle, Art. 5(2))** | ICO, ico.org.uk |
| Email/SMS to individual prospects requires consent or charitable soft opt-in | **Binding (PECR Reg. 22, as amended DUA 2025)** | ICO, ico.org.uk |
| Telephone calls to TPS-registered numbers require consent | **Binding (PECR Reg. 21)** | ICO, ico.org.uk |
| Postal outreach requires LI but no PECR consent | **Binding (UK GDPR only)** | ICO, ico.org.uk |
| Code of Fundraising Practice standards on due diligence and consent observed | Best practice (regulatory co-regulation) | Fundraising Regulator, fundraisingregulator.org.uk |
| CASE/CIoF LIA and DPIA documentation approach followed | Best practice | CASE, case.org; CIoF via FR briefings |

**The central lesson from 2016–2017 enforcement:** the processing is not automatically unlawful — it is unlawful when it is **undisclosed**. A charity that documents its LIA, completes a DPIA where triggered, maintains a clear privacy notice covering prospect research, executes Article 28 agreements with all screeners, and respects PECR channel rules is in a materially different position from the charities that were fined.
